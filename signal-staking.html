Signal vs staking

Two independent tests — is there a signal, and did the staking help — so a lucky run cannot top the board.

What this page is. Requirement 5 of the 2026-11-14 brief: the leaderboard now has two independent tests and two verdicts. The signal column asks whether a larger disagreement with the market predicts a better outcome. The staking column asks what the realised return was at the recorded price. A strategy can no longer rank high on return alone: the ranking is by signal evidence first, and a positive ROI with no signal evidence is labelled LUCK_RISK and placed below every real signal.

1 · Why the old leaderboard could be fooled

Ranked by ROI, the top two rows were PARLAY2_ML (+10.93% on 345 tickets) and WIND_UNDER (+9.15% on 456 bets). Both are the smallest samples in the library, and both sit beside SP_EDGE_1_0 (−1.88% on 3,941 bets). A 345-ticket sample cannot distinguish a 10% return from a 0% return at conventional confidence: the standard error of the mean return on 345 independent tickets with a ~1.6-unit Bernoulli payout is roughly 0.09, so the observed +0.109 is about 1.2 standard errors from zero. That is the exact failure mode the brief names — and it is now encoded, not narrated.

The two tests are deliberately different in kind:

  • Signal uses the model's own variable (`signal` = size of the disagreement) and the realised `pnl`, and reports a Spearman rank correlation with a weekly-block bootstrap interval. Whole weeks are resampled because bets on one slate are not independent.
  • Staking uses the recorded price: ROI with the same weekly-block interval, plus a flag that says whether every bet carried a real price at all.

Ranked by signal evidence, not by return. `level` records what is being tested: a bet for single-bet strategies, a ticket for parlays — a parlay's signal is a sum of leg signals, so its correlation measures the legs, not the ticket.

4 · The probability check behind every expected value

Two tests, and they are different questions. Alignment: the probability must be the one that belongs to the bet — the same side, and the same line the bet was taken at. Calibration: the probability must mean what it says; a model that claims 55% where the outcome happens 51% of the time produces an expected return that is only a measure of its own over-confidence.

The alignment check passes for every market and side: each bet was taken at the line its probability was computed at, and each probability moves with the outcome it describes. The calibration check does not pass on the model's own numbers, so the expectation used in the luck gap below is computed from a walk-forward recalibration: the mapping is fitted only on earlier seasons and applied to the season in hand, with the market's de-vigged price as a second feature, because the model's error on longshots is far larger than its average error. No game is calibrated with knowledge of a later result.

The luck gap. The gap between the realised return and the return the signal said to expect. A large positive gap is a strategy that got lucky; a large negative gap is a strategy whose edge was real and was not paid. The gap against the uncalibrated expectation is published beside it, and it is far larger — an expectation built from an over-confident probability reports the over-confidence rather than the luck, which is exactly the mistake this check exists to catch.
