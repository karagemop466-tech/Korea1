The framework, stated precisely

A player prop is a bet on a count. Pricing it needs three things, in this order:

  1. A per-game distribution for the player's count — not a mean. Receptions and carries are over-dispersed counts (negative binomial); yards are right-skewed and better modelled as a shifted lognormal or a gamma; touchdowns are near-Poisson with a huge zero mass.
  2. An availability model — a prop is void if the player does not play. Snap share and injury status drive both the mean and the void probability, and a model that ignores them is biased, not just noisy.
  3. The joint distribution across legs — this is where parlay pricing lives, and where almost every public SGP calculator quietly cheats by multiplying.

For a two-leg same-game parlay the fair price is P(A∩B) = P(A)·P(B) + Cov(A,B). If the legs are positively correlated and the book prices them independently, the book is underpricing the parlay — you should take it. What can be said without player data

The structural parents of prop markets are measurable here, and they are the part that usually goes wrong in public calculators:

  • Team scoring variance is large relative to its covariance: var(home) = 104.57, var(away) = 99.96, cov(home, away) = -3.98.
  • The home share of total variance is 0.532 — a team-total over is not simply "half a game-total over".
  • Game total sd is 14.02 points against a mean of 44.83.

Those three numbers are enough to price a team total honestly and to know how much a pair of opposing team-total legs co-move. They are not enough to price a player prop, which is why the player layer stays empty rather than invented.
